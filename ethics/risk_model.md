# Risk Model

The Culture Key Risk Model identifies structural conditions that may
undermine safe, legitimate, accountable, and human-preserving AI operation.

It is designed to detect risk before failure becomes the only visible signal.

Risk is not limited to harmful outputs.

It may also emerge from changes in authority, behavior, decision structure,
accountability, system interaction, or governance over time.

---

## Risk Model Purpose

The Risk Model is designed to help identify:

- what may cause harm or governance failure
- where authority may become unclear or exceed its mandate
- where human agency may be weakened
- where accountability may become difficult to reconstruct
- where system behavior or governance may drift from an approved baseline
- where existing safeguards may fail under scale, speed, complexity, or interaction

Detection of risk does not itself determine the governance response.

Risk must be evaluated according to its context, materiality, impact,
and applicable authority structure.

---

## Primary Risk Domains

### 1. Manipulation and Influence Risk

Risk that system behavior may improperly shape human judgment, behavior,
or choice.

Examples include:

- coercive or deceptive guidance
- exploitation of cognitive or emotional vulnerability
- hidden behavioral steering
- misleading framing designed to influence a decision
- optimization for compliance rather than informed choice

**Governance objective:** preserve meaningful human agency and prevent
unjustified influence.

---

### 2. Autonomy and Dependency Risk

Risk that human decision-making becomes structurally dependent on AI systems
or that meaningful human judgment is displaced.

Examples include:

- over-automation of sensitive decisions
- systematic substitution of AI judgment for human judgment
- inability to meaningfully question or override system recommendations
- dependency created through repeated delegation
- loss of practical human capability or decision ownership

**Governance objective:** preserve meaningful human judgment, choice,
and capacity to intervene.

---

### 3. Bias and Unequal Impact Risk

Risk that system behavior reproduces, amplifies, or creates unfair
patterns of treatment or impact.

Examples include:

- systematic bias propagation
- distorted recommendations
- unequal error distribution
- discriminatory outcomes
- feedback loops that reinforce existing disadvantage

**Governance objective:** identify and reduce unjustified differential
impact and amplification effects.

---

### 4. Opacity and Traceability Risk

Risk that decisions, actions, or governance pathways become difficult
to inspect, reconstruct, or challenge.

Examples include:

- unclear decision pathways
- missing or inadequate decision records
- inability to identify who or what authorized an action
- hidden state or rule transitions
- insufficient visibility into governance-relevant system behavior

**Governance objective:** preserve enough visibility to support
accountability, review, and contestability.

---

### 5. Accountability Risk

Risk that responsibility for system decisions or consequences becomes
unclear, fragmented, or practically unenforceable.

Examples include:

- unclear ownership of decisions
- responsibility distributed across actors without clear accountability
- missing review authority
- inability to identify who owns corrective action
- accountability gaps between technical and governance processes

**Governance objective:** ensure that responsibility and accountability
remain attributable throughout the decision lifecycle.

---

### 6. Authority Risk

Risk that an actor, agent, process, or governance role exercises authority
outside its legitimate mandate.

Examples include:

- autonomous expansion of decision scope
- intervention without defined authority
- escalation to an actor without the mandate to decide
- concentration of incompatible governance powers
- practical authority exceeding formally approved authority
- an authority reviewing or legitimizing its own contested scope

**Governance objective:** ensure that authority remains explicit,
bounded, legitimate, and reviewable.

---

### 7. Governance Drift Risk

Risk that system behavior, governance practice, or authority boundaries
gradually move away from an approved baseline without a single obvious
failure event.

Examples include:

- repeated exceptions becoming normal practice
- changing decision patterns
- increasing tolerance of boundary violations
- gradual expansion of actor authority
- safeguards becoming weaker through operational shortcuts
- governance rules remaining formally unchanged while practice changes

**Governance objective:** detect material deviation early enough for
review, correction, rollback, or revalidation.

---

### 8. Escalation and Resolution Risk

Risk that a governance-relevant case cannot reach the appropriate authority
or cannot be legitimately resolved.

Examples include:

- missing escalation destinations
- escalation paths that are too slow for the operational context
- routing to an authority without the required mandate
- unresolved conflicts between legitimate authorities
- absence of an arbitration path where ordinary escalation is insufficient
- contested decisions without meaningful review or resolution

**Governance objective:** preserve viable, timely, and legitimate paths
for escalation, review, and conflict resolution.

---

### 9. Intervention and Recovery Risk

Risk that a system cannot be meaningfully restricted, stopped, corrected,
or restored when governance boundaries are exceeded.

Examples include:

- intervention mechanisms that are too slow
- inability to pause or restrict relevant operation
- rollback mechanisms that do not restore a known acceptable state
- unclear authority to activate intervention
- recovery without appropriate revalidation
- technical stoppability without operational governance capacity

**Governance objective:** ensure that meaningful intervention and recovery
remain possible before, during, and after material failure.

---

### 10. Interaction and Emergent Risk

Risk that individually acceptable components or decisions create harmful
or ungovernable behavior when combined.

Examples include:

- multi-agent interaction producing unexpected authority patterns
- cascading decisions across systems
- feedback loops between automated actors
- distributed actions whose combined impact exceeds individual mandates
- local safeguards failing to control system-level behavior

**Governance objective:** evaluate risk at the level of the interacting
system, not only at the level of individual components.

---

## Risk Materiality

Not every detected signal requires the same response.

Risk materiality should be evaluated using factors such as:

- severity of potential impact
- likelihood or credibility
- number of people or systems potentially affected
- reversibility of consequences
- speed at which harm or governance failure may propagate
- uncertainty in available evidence
- whether protected rights or governance boundaries are implicated
- whether the risk is isolated or part of a recurring pattern
- whether existing controls remain effective

Materiality assessment must occur through an authorized evaluation process
appropriate to the type and impact of the risk.

---

## Risk Handling

Culture Key distinguishes between:

**Detection** — identifying a possible risk signal.

**Evaluation** — determining its context, credibility, and materiality.

**Governance Response** — selecting an authorized and proportionate response.

**Intervention** — restricting, pausing, blocking, correcting, or rolling back
operation where authorized and necessary.

**Escalation** — routing a decision when the current actor or process has
reached the limit of its legitimate authority.

**Resolution** — determining and recording the legitimate outcome,
including arbitration where ordinary governance paths are insufficient.

These functions should not be assumed to belong to the same actor.

---

## Risk Response Principles

Risk handling should be:

- proportionate to materiality and impact
- bounded by defined authority
- traceable where governance-relevant
- capable of meaningful escalation
- contestable where appropriate
- responsive to patterns as well as individual events
- capable of correction, rollback, and revalidation where required

A risk signal does not automatically justify maximum intervention.

Likewise, the absence of immediate harm does not establish the absence
of structural risk.

---

## Relationship to the Core Governance Architecture

The Risk Model supports the risk and evidence evaluation function
within the Culture Key Core Governance Architecture.

It does not determine governance outcomes on its own.

Risk signals and materiality assessments inform state and decision logic,
but resulting actions must remain subject to legitimate authority,
accountability, intervention boundaries, escalation, arbitration,
traceability, and revalidation where applicable.

The Risk Model therefore provides structured governance evidence.

It is not a standalone decision authority.


---

## Final Principle

A system should not be considered well-governed merely because harmful
outcomes have not yet occurred.

Governance must also ask whether authority remains legitimate,
human agency remains meaningful, accountability remains traceable,
intervention remains possible, and emerging patterns remain governable
as the system changes and scales.
