# Governance Metrics

Culture Key uses metrics to observe whether governance mechanisms remain
effective, legitimate, responsive, and capable of correction over time.

Metrics are governance signals.

They are not substitutes for judgment, evidence, review, or legitimate
decision authority.

A metric should not be treated as proof that a system is safe merely
because it remains within a numerical threshold.

---

## Purpose

Governance metrics should help identify:

- emerging risk patterns
- behavioral or governance drift
- authority failures
- escalation bottlenecks
- intervention and recovery weaknesses
- repeated exceptions
- accountability gaps
- changes in the effectiveness of safeguards

Metrics should support better governance questions.

They should not create false certainty.

---

## Metric Design Principles

Governance-relevant metrics should be:

- connected to a defined governance purpose
- interpretable in context
- proportionate to the risk being observed
- traceable to sufficiently reliable evidence
- reviewed when the system or operating context changes
- resistant to becoming performance targets that distort behavior

Where a metric influences a governance decision, the authority to interpret
and act upon that metric should be defined.

Measurement does not itself create decision authority.

---

## Risk Signal Metrics

Possible indicators include:

- frequency of detected risk signals
- distribution across risk domains
- severity or materiality patterns
- recurring combinations of risk signals
- rate of previously unseen or unclassified cases
- concentration of risk within specific components or interaction paths

Increases or decreases should not be interpreted automatically as improvement
or deterioration.

For example, increased risk detection may reflect worsening system behavior,
improved detection capability, changed usage, or changed classification rules.

Context matters.

---

## Review Quality Metrics

Possible indicators include:

- proportion of reviewed decisions later reversed or materially corrected
- disagreement rates between independent reviewers where applicable
- unresolved review cases
- repeated reconsideration of the same decision class
- time required for review relative to operational risk
- cases where available evidence was insufficient for legitimate resolution

The objective is not to minimize review.

It is to identify whether review remains meaningful, timely, and capable
of correcting error.

---

## Authority Integrity Metrics

Possible indicators include:

- decisions taken outside approved authority boundaries
- attempted unauthorized interventions
- cases routed to an authority without the required mandate
- repeated ambiguity about decision ownership
- frequency of authority-boundary disputes
- cases where detection, evaluation, approval, and review authority were
  improperly collapsed

Authority integrity should be assessed through both individual events
and recurring patterns.

A low number of detected authority violations does not by itself demonstrate
that authority boundaries are functioning correctly.

---

## Escalation Metrics

Possible indicators include:

- number and type of escalations
- escalation destination
- time to reach an authorized decision-maker or mechanism
- escalations routed incorrectly
- cases returned because the receiving authority lacked mandate
- unresolved escalations
- repeated escalation of the same issue class
- cases requiring arbitration

Escalation volume should not be treated as a success or failure metric
in isolation.

Too few escalations may indicate unresolved boundary violations.

Too many may indicate unclear authority, weak rules, or an overly narrow
operational mandate.

---

## Drift Metrics

Possible indicators include:

- changes in decision patterns over time
- increasing exception frequency
- repeated movement toward governance boundaries
- changes in state-transition patterns
- practical expansion of authority
- weakening or bypassing of safeguards
- repeated temporary measures becoming normal practice
- differences between documented governance and operational behavior

Drift should be evaluated against an explicit baseline where possible.

Not every change is drift.

Drift becomes governance-relevant when change materially affects approved
behavior, authority, safeguards, accountability, or protected assumptions.

---

## Intervention and Stop Metrics

Possible indicators include:

- time from material detection to authorized intervention
- successful and unsuccessful stop attempts
- interventions activated without proper authority
- interventions delayed by unclear ownership
- scope of operation successfully restricted
- attempts to bypass or circumvent intervention mechanisms
- mismatch between required and available intervention speed

The existence of a stop mechanism is not sufficient.

Its operational effectiveness must be observable.

---

## Recovery and Revalidation Metrics

Possible indicators include:

- rollback success and failure
- time to establish a known acceptable operating condition
- recovery attempts requiring repeated correction
- systems returned to operation without required revalidation
- failures discovered after restoration
- differences between pre-failure and restored governance conditions
- time between recovery and authorized re-entry into normal operation

Recovery speed should not be optimized independently of recovery integrity.

Fast restoration is not successful restoration if the system remains
outside its legitimate governance baseline.

---

## Accountability Metrics

Possible indicators include:

- governance-relevant decisions without identifiable ownership
- cases without a defined accountable authority
- incomplete decision or intervention records
- unresolved corrective actions
- repeated failures assigned without effective remediation
- cases where independent review was required but unavailable

Accountability metrics should help identify structural gaps,
not merely count documented actors.

---

## Emergent and Interaction Metrics

Where systems contain multiple agents, models, or automated processes,
possible indicators include:

- unexpected interaction pathways
- cascading actions
- repeated cross-agent escalation
- combined actions exceeding individual mandates
- feedback loops associated with governance-relevant behavior
- failures visible only at system level
- propagation speed across components

Component-level compliance should not be treated as proof of system-level
governability.

---

## Metric Baselines and Thresholds

Thresholds should not be universal by default.

Where quantitative thresholds are used, they should be justified according to:

- operational context
- risk domain
- expected baseline
- severity and reversibility
- system scale and propagation speed
- quality and uncertainty of available data

Thresholds that trigger governance action should be versioned and reviewed.

A threshold crossing may trigger evaluation.

It should not automatically determine the final governance decision unless
such authority and behavior have been explicitly defined.

---

## Metric Drift

Metrics themselves may become unreliable.

Metric drift may occur when:

- system behavior changes
- detection methods change
- classifications are redefined
- usage patterns shift
- incentives change because a metric becomes a target
- data quality deteriorates
- the operating environment changes

Metrics should therefore be periodically reviewed for continued validity.

A governance system should not assume that yesterday's indicator still
measures tomorrow's risk.

---

## Privacy and Anti-Profiling

Governance measurement does not justify unnecessary user surveillance.

Metrics should be designed, where possible, around:

- system behavior
- governance events
- decision pathways
- authority use
- aggregate patterns
- safety and recovery performance

rather than persistent behavioral profiles of individual users.

Where person-linked information is genuinely necessary for a legitimate
governance or protective purpose, its use should follow the applicable
privacy, proportionality, authority, and retention requirements.

---

## Governance Use

Metrics may inform:

- risk evaluation
- governance review
- drift investigation
- policy revision
- authority redesign
- intervention planning
- revalidation
- versioned governance change

Metrics inform these processes.

They do not replace them.

---

## Final Principle

What is measurable is not automatically what matters most.

And what matters most is not always directly measurable.

Culture Key uses metrics to make governance more observable —
not to reduce governance to a dashboard.
