# Consent Protocol

The Culture Key Consent Protocol defines how meaningful human choice,
permission, participation, and withdrawal should be respected in
AI-mediated interactions.

Consent is an important governance mechanism.

It is not the sole source of legitimate authority for every system action.

The purpose of this protocol is to preserve meaningful human agency while
distinguishing consent from other legitimate governance, safety, legal,
or protective authorities.

---

## Consent Principles

Valid consent should be:

- informed
- sufficiently specific to the relevant purpose
- freely given where genuine choice exists
- understandable
- revocable where withdrawal is applicable
- distinguishable from coercion, manipulation, or forced acceptance

A system should not describe participation as consensual where meaningful
refusal is not actually available.

---

## Consent and Human Agency

Consent should preserve meaningful choice rather than merely record agreement.

Where consent is required, the person should be able to understand, at a
level appropriate to the interaction:

- what they are agreeing to
- the relevant purpose
- what system or process is involved
- what material consequences may follow
- what data or permissions are relevant
- whether and how consent may be withdrawn

Interface acceptance alone does not establish meaningful consent.

---

## Consent Scope

Consent should be bounded to the purpose and context for which it was given.

Consent to one activity should not automatically be treated as consent to:

- unrelated data use
- materially different system behavior
- expanded processing purposes
- new forms of automated action
- persistent profiling
- disclosure to additional actors
- governance changes that materially alter the original participation context

Material expansion beyond the original scope may require renewed consent
where consent is the applicable basis.

---

## Withdrawal and Disengagement

Where consent is revocable, withdrawal should be meaningful in practice.

Systems should define:

- how consent may be withdrawn
- what processing or interaction stops
- what cannot reasonably or legally be reversed
- what information may need to be retained and why
- how safe disengagement occurs

Withdrawal should not create unjustified penalty or coercive pressure
to remain in the interaction.

Withdrawal of consent does not necessarily invalidate actions that were
legitimately completed before withdrawal.

---

## Consent Is Not Unlimited Authority

User consent does not authorize every action.

A person cannot grant a system legitimate authority to:

- violate protected rights of others
- bypass governance-critical constraints
- override applicable legal obligations
- expand an actor's mandate beyond its authorized scope
- remove required accountability or safety controls

Consent should therefore be evaluated within the wider governance structure.

**Consent cannot legitimize what the consenting person has no authority
to authorize.**

---

## Action Without Consent

Some actions may have a legitimate basis other than consent.

Depending on context, these may include:

- protection against credible and serious risk
- actions necessary to preserve system or operational integrity
- compliance with applicable legal obligations
- protection of the rights or safety of others
- authorized governance or security intervention

Such action should not be described as consensual.

Where consent is not the applicable basis, the actual authority or
justification should be identifiable.

Non-consensual action should remain:

- necessary for a legitimate purpose
- proportionate to that purpose
- bounded by applicable authority
- traceable where governance-relevant
- reviewable where appropriate
- limited in scope and duration where applicable

The absence of consent does not create unlimited protective authority.

---

## Data and Purpose Boundaries

Data obtained through a consensual interaction should remain subject
to the declared purpose and applicable governance boundaries.

Systems should avoid:

- silent repurposing
- unnecessary collection
- unjustified retention
- persistent behavioral profiling without legitimate basis
- expansion of data use merely because technical capability exists

Where data use materially changes, the system should determine whether
renewed consent, another legitimate basis, or governance review is required.

---

## Sensitive and High-Impact Contexts

Consent may require additional safeguards where:

- power between parties is significantly asymmetric
- refusal may carry meaningful consequences
- the person may be vulnerable to coercion or manipulation
- decisions have significant or irreversible effects
- sensitive information is involved
- automated systems materially shape available choices

In such contexts, formal agreement should not automatically be treated
as evidence of meaningful consent.

---

## AI Attribution

Where relevant to meaningful choice, people should be able to understand
when they are interacting with AI or when AI materially contributes
to an outcome.

AI involvement should not be intentionally obscured where that information
would materially affect informed participation, accountability, or trust.

Attribution requirements should remain proportionate to context and impact.

---

## Consent Over Time

Consent may lose validity when the conditions under which it was given
materially change.

Relevant changes may include:

- significant change in purpose
- material change in system behavior
- new data use
- expanded authority or automation
- materially different consequences
- governance changes affecting the original participation conditions

Versioning and change-control processes should therefore consider whether
a material change requires renewed consent.

---

## Traceability

Where consent is governance-relevant, records should make it possible
to determine, proportionate to context:

- what was consented to
- for what purpose
- under which relevant conditions
- when consent was given
- whether it was modified or withdrawn
- whether later system changes affected its validity

Consent records should not become a justification for unnecessary
surveillance or indefinite data retention.

---

## Relationship to Authority

Consent, authority, and accountability are related but distinct.

**Consent** establishes meaningful permission where permission is required.

**Authority** determines who or what has the legitimate mandate to act.

**Accountability** determines who remains answerable for the action
and its consequences.

None of these should be assumed to substitute automatically for the others.

---

## Design Principle

Consent should be treated as an ongoing governance condition,
not as a one-time checkbox.

Its purpose is not merely to prove that agreement occurred.

Its purpose is to preserve meaningful human choice within legitimate
governance boundaries.
